01

Background

Project purpose

Develop and validate a single-axis embedded servo controller representative of an industrial precision-motion application.

Background

This project grew out of a conversation with a former coworker at a wafer-dicing company. Their team is considering developing an in-house motion controller for a machine incorporating an XYθ stage and coordinated laser-galvo control. This FOC servo controller is a single-axis proof of concept designed around that use case. The original performance targets followed common industrial servo practice: a 20kHz current-control update rate and approximately 500Hz current-loop bandwidth.

Project phases

  1. Hardware bring-up: connect the motor, encoder, gate driver, and Raspberry Pi 5. Develop the embedded firmware, then run the control algorithms against a simulated motor plant before applying them to real hardware.
  2. Motor characterization: with the motor unloaded, identify its electrical and mechanical dynamics, design the current, velocity, and position controllers, and verify their performance against measured hardware data.
  3. Stage integration: connect the motor to a ball-screw stage, characterize the additional inertia, friction, compliance, and backlash, and redesign and validate the controllers for the complete mechanical system.

Development began at the innermost loop and progressed outward: current, velocity, and then position. For each loop, the results show the measured or modeled plant, the controller designed against it, and the measured closed-loop response. Loop-gain crossover frequency and phase margin are evaluated for each loop as well, backed out from the closed-loop measurement.

The current loop runs at 20kHz. Each outer loop targets 60° of phase margin and a bandwidth at least three to five times lower than the loop beneath it.

The original current-loop bandwidth target was 500Hz. This was reduced to 250Hz after the higher controller gain began amplifying high-frequency current-sense noise. The leading suspect is the prototype shunt current-sensing hardware, which currently uses jumper-wire connections and is not laid out optimally. This remains a separate investigation, although several hardware and firmware changes have already improved the measurements.

All plant measurements were obtained from frequency-response sweeps performed on the hardware, with the captured data analyzed offline in Python.

Current loop. The motor's electrical transfer function was initially calculated from its datasheet resistance and inductance, then checked against a measured frequency response. A PI controller was designed using pole-zero cancellation with a target bandwidth of 250Hz. The loop-gain response was evaluated for crossover frequency and phase margin, and a closed-loop sweep was performed to verify the achieved bandwidth and current tracking.

Velocity loop. The velocity plant was measured with the current loop closed underneath it. A direct open-loop sweep produced unreliable results below approximately 10Hz, suspected friction. To improve low-frequency characterization, the velocity loop was first stabilized with a proportional controller. Its closed-loop transfer function was measured, and the underlying plant was backed out from the known controller and closed-loop response. A PI velocity controller was then designed using pole-zero cancellation, targeting an 80Hz bandwidth. The completed loop was swept again to verify its closed-loop response.

Position loop. The position-loop plant combines the measured closed-loop velocity response with the integration from velocity to position. Because the plant already contains an integrator, a proportional controller is sufficient to set the position loop bandwidth. The loop gain was evaluated for crossover frequency and phase margin. A closed-loop frequency sweep and position-step test were then used to verify bandwidth, tracking accuracy, overshoot, and settling behavior.

02

Hardware & firmware architecture

Everything below runs on hardware and firmware built from scratch for this project: an STM32F411 driving a TI DRV8353RS gate driver, with a Raspberry Pi 5 as a telemetry sink and offline analysis host. No vendor motor-control SDK: the ISR structure, PWM/ADC synchronization, and all three control loops are custom, not provided by a vendor framework.

Actuation & sensing (STM32F411)

TIM1 center-aligned PWM (CMS=01) at 20kHz drives the DRV8353's three half-bridges. Phase currents come back through an ADC1 injected conversion group, hardware-triggered off the same PWM timer and serviced in an ISR, so current sampling is phase-locked to the switching edge, not free-running. Rotor position is an incremental quadrature encoder read entirely in hardware by TIM2's encoder-mode counter (both edges, 32-bit, no software pulse counting), signal-conditioned through a line driver.

Control & comms

Three nested loops share the 20kHz ISR: current every tick, velocity every 4th (5kHz), position every 20th (1kHz). The DRV8353 gate driver is configured and monitored over SPI1. A second SPI bus free-runs telemetry to the Raspberry Pi 5, which does all offline system ID and Bode analysis in Python (Welch/CSD cross-spectral estimation, nonlinear least-squares fits). Nothing below runs on-device.

Phase 2

Motor-Only Servo Drive Conclusion

The cascade behaves well: ~1% overshoot into a 2% settling band in ~36ms. The current loop runs at a verified 255Hz, confirmed sufficient rather than assumed: pushing it to 500Hz and 1000Hz live on hardware left every outer loop's measured margin unchanged, while introducing real risk (audible noise, one genuine instability event) for no benefit, so the proven 255Hz gain stays. The real ceiling on outer-loop bandwidth is the backlash-modulated mechanical resonance found near 65–90Hz, not current-loop speed.

Phase 3: friction, feedforward, and re-closing the loops

The motor is now driving a real linear stage (150mm travel, 2mm lead/rev) instead of spinning free. Friction was a problem: the same closed velocity-loop chirp, run at two different command amplitudes (8 vs 15 rad/s), backed out plant gain/time-constant numbers that differed by nearly 2x - a supposedly linear measurement that depended on signal amplitude. This section is that investigation, the fix, and the redesigned position loop verified against it.

During constant-velocity motion, the proportional position loop produces the finite ramp-following error expected from a type-1 servo. With Kp,pos=205.5 s-1 and approximately unity velocity-loop DC gain, the predicted following error is v/Kp: 48.7µm at 10mm/s and 4.87µm at 1mm/s. The measured values, 54.7µm (held steady through nearly the entire cruise, 99th percentile 53.5µm, not a transient spike) and ~5.0µm, closely match the model. Velocity feedforward is the natural next step for reducing this error without increasing position-loop gain.

The velocity trace also showed real ripple on the cruise (std ≈0.23–0.35mm/s). FFT'd it rather than guessing: at 10mm/s the peaks formed a harmonic family near 35–80Hz, and 5Hz is exactly the mechanical rotation rate at that speed, suggestive of cogging, encoder eccentricity, or a lead-screw pitch imperfection (all position-synchronous). Re-ran the same move at 1mm/s to test that hypothesis directly: a position-synchronous source should drop the peaks 10x, to ~0.5–8Hz. They didn't; they stayed in almost the same 40–70Hz range. That rules out the mechanical hypothesis and points instead at the velocity loop's own ~107Hz closed-loop bandwidth shaping the already-characterized broadband shunt-current noise into a hump near its crossover, the same mechanism as the position-loop "resonance" above, one loop level down.

Phase 3

Load-Compensated Servo Drive Conclusion

Working position loop with every number measured against the loaded system: friction curve, feedforward, re-verified linearity, closed-loop margin, step response, and a resonance-looking artifact correctly traced back to loop margin instead of hardware, including reporting a margin (39°) that came in short of the 60° target, not just the wins.

Shunt current-sense noise (flagged in Section 8 as the clear next step) was measured directly: ~130mA std at zero commanded current, about 30x the ADC's own quantization noise. A controlled on/off comparison isolated one real contributor: the oscilloscope probe's ground clip injecting noise through a ground loop. Removing it cut the floor to ~85–95mA std. Still open: the ADC's injected-conversion trigger isn't yet synchronized to a quiet point in the PWM cycle, which is the leading candidate for the rest of it. Also still open from the original plan: a Kalman velocity filter was not built; instead, a real (different) bug was found and fixed in the existing velocity telemetry path (an int16_t cast overflowing above 25 rad/s), which was a genuine problem but not the one originally planned for.
